# Electric Power Generation, Transmission and Distribution

The electric power industry (NAICS 2211) comprises the generation of electrical energy from various sources, transmission over high-voltage networks, and distribution to end-use customers. This critical infrastructure sector operates as a highly regulated, capital-intensive industry requiring continuous real-time balancing of supply and demand across interconnected regional grids.

## Overview

The electric utility value chain consists of three main vertically-integrated segments, though deregulation has enabled independent operations:

- **Generation (APQC 10.0)**: Producing electricity from fossil fuels, nuclear, renewable, and other sources
- **Transmission (APQC 11.0)**: Moving power over long distances via high-voltage networks (69 kV to 765 kV)
- **Distribution (APQC 11.0-11.2)**: Delivering power to customers via local networks (typically <69 kV)

### Industry Scale (U.S., 2024)

- **Generation Capacity**: ~1,200 GW (peak demand ~850 GW)
- **Annual Generation**: ~4,200 TWh
- **Transmission Lines**: 700,000+ circuit miles
- **Distribution Lines**: 5.5 million+ miles
- **Total Substations**: 55,000+ (transmission and distribution)
- **Customers**: 150+ million residential, commercial, industrial
- **Grid Reliability**: 99.95%+ uptime (SAIDI: ~1.5 hours/year)
- **Market Value**: $700+ billion (generation, transmission, distribution)
- **Employees**: 400,000+ (generation, transmission, distribution, retail)

### Market Participants

**Vertically-Integrated Utilities (IOUs)**
- Own generation, transmission, and distribution assets
- Serve ~70% of U.S. electric customers
- Examples: Duke Energy, Southern Company, NextEra, American Electric Power

**Public Power Utilities**
- Municipally-owned utilities and public power districts
- Serve ~15% of U.S. customers
- Often operate only distribution assets

**Cooperatives**
- Member-owned utilities
- Serve ~10% of customers
- Mix of generation, transmission, and distribution ownership

**Merchant Generators**
- Own only generation assets
- Operate in deregulated markets
- Compete in wholesale energy markets (RTOs/ISOs)

**Transmission Companies (TransCos)**
- Own transmission assets only
- Regulated utility operations in deregulated markets

**Electric Retailers**
- Purchase power and resell to customers in deregulated markets
- Emerging in Texas, Northeast, and other competitive regions

## Generation Technologies (APQC 10.0)

### Fossil Fuel Generation

**Natural Gas Combined Cycle (NGCC) - NAICS 221112**
- Gas turbine + steam turbine configuration
- Efficiency: 50-60% (vs. 33-38% for simple cycle)
- Fast ramping capability: 30-60 minutes to full power
- Lower emissions than coal (50% less CO2 per kWh)
- Capacity factor: 50-60%
- Capital cost: $1,000-1,200/kW (2024)
- Operating cost: $30-50/MWh (2024, fuel dependent)
- U.S. capacity: ~450 GW

**Coal-Fired Steam Plants - NAICS 221112**
- Pulverized coal boilers with steam turbines
- Efficiency: 33-40% (subcritical), 40-45% (supercritical)
- Baseload operation, slow ramping (4-8 hours to full power)
- Emissions controls: SCR (NOx), FGD (SO2), ESP (particulates), CCS (emerging)
- Capacity factor: 40-50% (declining due to retirements)
- Coal ash handling and disposal requirements
- Operating cost: $40-60/MWh
- U.S. capacity: ~200 GW (declining)

**Peaking/Simple Cycle Gas Turbines**
- Gas turbine only (no heat recovery)
- Rapid start capability: 10-20 minutes to full power
- Lower efficiency: 25-35%
- Low capital cost: $700-900/kW
- Capacity factor: 5-15%
- Operating cost: $80-120/MWh
- Used for peak demand and reserve capacity

**Oil-Fired Generation (declining)**
- Steam and combustion turbine plants
- Primarily used as peaking units or in isolated regions
- Operating cost: $120-150/MWh (higher fuel cost)

### Nuclear Power - NAICS 221113

**Light Water Reactors (LWRs)**
- Pressurized Water Reactors (PWR): 65% of U.S. reactors
- Boiling Water Reactors (BWR): 35% of U.S. reactors
- Typical capacity: 1,000-1,400 MW per unit
- Capacity factor: 92%+ (highest among all technologies)
- Fuel: Enriched uranium (3-5% U-235)
- Operating license: 40 years; many extended to 60-80 years
- Operating cost: $30-40/MWh (fuel and operation only)
- Capital cost: $10-20 billion per reactor (2020s), $10-15/W
- Refueling cycles: 18-24 months
- U.S. capacity: ~95 GW from ~93 operating reactors

**Nuclear Operations**
- Strict Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) oversight
- Security: Armed guards, protected areas, cyber defense
- Emergency core cooling systems (ECCS) and containment
- Spent fuel storage: on-site pools and dry casks
- Decommissioning: 40-60 year process after shutdown
- Waste disposal: Yucca Mountain project remains unresolved

**Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)**
- Capacity: 50-300 MW per module
- Factory fabrication, modular construction, scalable
- Passive safety systems (walk-away safe)
- NuScale (first U.S. design certification 2020)
- Early commercialization: 2028+
- Applications: Remote locations, industrial heat, desalination
- Capital cost: Higher per-MW initially, economies of scale expected

### Renewable Energy Generation

**Hydroelectric Power - NAICS 221111**
- Conventional dams: Impoundment reservoirs with generation
- Run-of-river: Minimal storage, continuous flow generation
- Pumped storage: Hydroelectric energy storage (charge/discharge)
- Capacity factor: 30-50% (varies by hydrology and rainfall)
- Operating cost: $10-30/MWh (minimal fuel cost)
- Capital cost: $1,500-7,500/kW (varies by site)
- Licensing: FERC hydropower licensing, 30-50 year licenses
- Environmental concerns: Fish passage, minimum flows, habitat impacts
- U.S. capacity: ~80 GW conventional, ~23 GW pumped storage
- Existing dams: Aging infrastructure, relicensing challenges

**Wind Power - NAICS 221115**
- Onshore turbines: 2.5-5.0 MW typical (up to 12-15 MW emerging)
  - Hub height: 80-120 m (increasing for better wind resources)
  - Rotor diameter: 90-220 m
  - Capacity factor: 25-35% (varies by location, class, technology)
  - Capital cost: $1,200-1,600/kW (2024)
  - Operating cost: $30-60/MWh (includes service contracts)

- Offshore turbines: 8-15 MW (14-18 MW emerging)
  - Higher wind resources: 40-50% capacity factor
  - Capital cost: $3,000-4,000/kW (floating: $4,000-5,000+)
  - Installation: Monopile, jacket, or floating foundation
  - Operating cost: $60-100/MWh (higher maintenance)
  - Depth: 30-300 m (floating extends to deeper water)

- Curtailment: Reducing wind output during low demand
- Production Tax Credit (PTC): $26/MWh (2024), declining
- U.S. capacity: ~140 GW (2024), growing ~12 GW/year
- Global market: ~1,200 GW (2024)

**Solar Photovoltaic (PV) - NAICS 221114**
- Utility-scale: >5 MW, fixed tilt or single-axis/dual-axis tracking
- Distributed: Rooftop, community solar, <10 MW
- Technology: Crystalline silicon (95% of market), thin film, perovskite (emerging)
- Efficiency: 20-23% (monocrystalline), 18-20% (polycrystalline), 15-18% (thin film)
- Capacity factor: 15-25% (varies by location, technology, tracking)
- Inverters: String, central, hybrid (battery-integrated), microinverters
- Capital cost: $800-1,200/kW (utility-scale), $1,200-2,000/kW (residential)
- Operating cost: $10-20/MWh
- Investment Tax Credit (ITC): 30% (2024-2032), declining 2033+
- U.S. capacity: ~100 GW utility-scale, ~40+ GW distributed (2024)
- Global capacity: ~1,000+ GW (2024)

**Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) - NAICS 221114**
- Parabolic troughs: Most deployed technology
- Power towers (central receiver): Newest deployments
- Linear Fresnel: Compact design alternative
- Thermal storage: Molten salt enables dispatchability
- Capacity factor: 25-35% (higher with 8-15 hour storage)
- Operating cost: $50-100/MWh (including storage)
- Capital cost: $4,000-6,000/kW (with storage)
- Primarily in southwestern U.S. (California, Arizona, Nevada)
- Global capacity: ~6+ GW (declining due to PV competition)

**Geothermal - NAICS 221116**
- Flash steam plants: High-temperature resources (>150°C)
- Binary cycle plants: Lower-temperature resources (70-150°C)
- Direct use: Heating/cooling, industrial processes
- Capacity factor: 70-90%+ (baseload renewable)
- Operating cost: $40-80/MWh (minimal fuel cost)
- Capital cost: $2,000-6,000/kW
- Resources: Western U.S. (California, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, Oregon)
- U.S. capacity: ~3.7 GW (minimal growth due to resource availability)
- Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS): Emerging, first commercial projects

**Biomass - NAICS 221117**
- Feedstock: Wood waste, agricultural residues, dedicated energy crops
- Technologies: Combustion, gasification, anaerobic digestion, biogas
- Capacity factor: 60-80% (biomass availability dependent)
- Operating cost: $50-100/MWh (fuel cost high, but waste/byproduct credit)
- Carbon considerations: Renewable but emits CO2; depends on feedstock sourcing
- U.S. capacity: ~14 GW (relatively stable)
- Co-generation: Paper mills and lumber operations

### Generation Mix Dynamics

**Capacity Mix (U.S., 2024)**
- Natural Gas: ~45% (declining share, growing GW due to retirements)
- Coal: ~20% (rapidly declining)
- Nuclear: ~9%
- Hydroelectric: ~7%
- Wind: ~13% (rapidly growing)
- Solar: ~5% (rapidly growing)
- Other/Biomass: ~1%

**Energy Mix (U.S., 2024)**
- Natural Gas: ~40%
- Coal: ~18%
- Nuclear: ~19%
- Hydroelectric: ~6%
- Wind: ~11%
- Solar: ~4%
- Other: ~2%

**Decarbonization Trajectory**
- Coal retirements: 10-15 GW/year (accelerating)
- Renewable additions: 30-40 GW/year
- Nuclear: New builds and license extensions
- Electrification of transport and heating increases demand

## Generation Operations (APQC 10.1-10.5)

### Generation Dispatch and Optimization (APQC 10.2)

**Merit Order Dispatch**
- Economic dispatch based on marginal cost
- Lowest variable cost units dispatched first
- Typical order: Hydro → Nuclear → Coal → NGCC → Peakers
- Renewable energy: Zero marginal cost, priority dispatch (must-run status)
- Real-time balancing: Every 5-15 minute dispatch cycle
- Reserve requirements: Spinning (10 minutes), non-spinning (30 minutes)

**Unit Commitment (APQC 10.1)**
- Day-ahead (24 hour) scheduling of generation units
- Hour-ahead (hourly) fine-tuning based on forecasts
- Real-time (5-15 minute) final dispatch
- Optimization objectives: Minimize cost, meet demand, maintain reserves
- Constraints: Ramp rates, minimum run times, minimum load, start-up costs
- Security-Constrained Unit Commitment (SCUC): Network flow limits

**Automatic Generation Control (AGC) - APQC 11.4**
- Real-time frequency regulation (maintain 60.00 Hz ±0.05 Hz)
- Area Control Error (ACE) monitoring and correction
- Economic dispatch signal every 4-10 seconds
- Ancillary services: Regulation up/down, spinning reserve
- Automatic response required within seconds

**Renewable Integration and Forecasting (APQC 10.5, 11.5)**
- Variability: Wind/solar output changes with weather (minutes to hours)
- Day-ahead forecasting: Persistence and numerical weather prediction (NWP)
- Hour-ahead forecasting: Higher accuracy for near-term management
- Forecast error: ±5-10% typical (day-ahead for solar, ±10-20% for wind)
- Curtailment: Reducing renewable output during oversupply
- Ramp rate management: Limiting generation changes per 5/15-minute interval
- Flexibility: Fast-ramping resources (gas, hydro, batteries) for balance

### Operations and Maintenance (APQC 10.3)

**Preventive Maintenance**
- Scheduled outages: Planned maintenance windows
- Inspection frequency: Annual to multi-year cycles
- Turbine/generator: Rotor balancing, bearing inspection, electrical tests
- Boiler/heat exchanger: Scale cleaning, tube inspection, water treatment
- Transformer: Oil analysis, cooling system maintenance, voltage regulation
- Balance-of-plant: Cooling towers, piping, condensers, pumps

**Predictive Maintenance**
- Vibration monitoring: Rotating equipment (turbines, generators, compressors)
- Thermography: Electrical connections, transformer hot spots
- Oil/coolant analysis: Particle count, acid number, moisture, metals
- Acoustic monitoring: Steam leaks, bearing defects, cavitation
- Prognostics: Remaining useful life (RUL) estimation
- Condition-based maintenance: Prevent failures before they occur

**Reliability and Availability (APQC 10.3)**
- Equivalent Availability Factor (EAF): Availability for economic dispatch
- Forced Outage Rate (FOR): Unplanned failures per unit time
- Planned Maintenance Rate (PMR): Scheduled maintenance availability
- Capacity factor: Actual generation vs. rated capacity
- Heat rate monitoring: Thermal efficiency (BTU/kWh)
- Emissions monitoring: CEMS (Continuous Emissions Monitoring)
- Data collection: NERC, EIA reporting requirements

## Transmission (APQC 11.1-11.4)

### Transmission Infrastructure

**High-Voltage Networks**
- Extra-High Voltage (EHV): 230 kV, 345 kV
- Ultra-High Voltage (UHV): 500 kV, 765 kV
- Function: Long-distance bulk power transfer
- Interconnection: Between utilities and regions
- Network: Three interconnections (Eastern, Western, Texas)
- Redundancy: Mesh network with alternative paths

**Substations**
- Step-up substations: Generation to transmission voltage
- Switching substations: Network reconfiguration
- Step-down substations: Transmission to distribution voltage (typically 35-138 kV)
- Equipment: Power transformers, circuit breakers, disconnect switches, bus, insulators

**HVDC (High-Voltage Direct Current)**
- Long-distance transmission: 500+ miles with lower losses (~0.6%/100 miles vs. 1%+ AC)
- Asynchronous interconnection: Different frequency systems (e.g., 50 Hz to 60 Hz)
- Submarine cables: Undersea and inland river crossings
- Converter stations: AC/DC and DC/AC conversion (6-10% efficiency loss)
- Examples: Pacific DC Intertie (3,100 MW), Quebec-New England (2,000 MW)
- Emerging technology: Multi-terminal HVDC, High-Voltage Direct Current
- Cost: Higher than AC for short distances, lower for long distances

**Transmission Line Types**
- Overhead: Steel towers, aluminum conductors, advantages in cost and ease of maintenance
- Underground: Cables in conduits, advantages in aesthetics, disadvantages in cost and cooling
- Submarine: Cable systems for offshore generation and interconnections

### Transmission Operations (APQC 11.1, 11.3-11.4)

**System Operator and Control**
- Real-time operation: 24/7/365 control center operations
- Security monitoring: Real-time impedance data via SCADA
- Voltage control: Reactive power management to maintain voltages
- Congestion management: Redispatch units to relieve constraints
- Emergency protocols: Islanding, load shedding, frequency support
- Communications: Fiber optic networks for control signals

**Grid Planning and Investment**
- 5-10 year capital plans: Substation upgrades, new lines, replacements
- Forecast demand: Load growth and peak demand projections
- Resource planning: Generation retirement and new resource integration
- Reliability assessment: N-1 contingency analysis
- Resilience: Storm hardening, undergrounding, microgrids
- Regulatory approval: State and FERC planning processes

**Maintenance and Asset Management**
- Inspection: Aerial inspection by helicopter, UAV, or ground crews
- Vegetation management: Tree trimming to prevent contact
- Component replacement: Transformers, breakers, conductors (useful life: 30-50 years)
- Predictive maintenance: Acoustic monitoring for corona, thermal imaging
- Emergency response: Storm recovery, equipment failures

## Distribution (APQC 11.2, 12.0)

### Distribution Infrastructure

**Distribution Network Hierarchy**
- Transmission-to-distribution: 138 kV, 69 kV (sub-transmission)
- Primary distribution: 12-35 kV (long distance within service territory)
- Secondary distribution: 120-480 V (customer level, residential 120/240V)
- Service lines: From pole to customer premise

**Distribution Assets**
- Poles: Wood (most common), concrete, steel; useful life: 40-70 years
- Transformers: Step-down (kV to 120/240V), efficiency: 97-99%
- Conductors: Copper, aluminum (bare or insulated)
- Switches: Load break, reclosers for automation
- Capacitor banks: Power factor correction and voltage support
- Meter infrastructure: Traditional electromechanical (declining), smart meters (growing)

**Distribution Automation (APQC 11.2)**
- SCADA: Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition
- Volt/VAR optimization: Automated voltage and reactive power control
- Fault location and isolation: Rapidly identify and isolate faults
- Automated feeder reconfiguration: Optimize power flow and reliability
- Demand response signals: Real-time pricing or load control signals
- Microgrid capability: Island and synchronize microgrids

### Customer Service and Billing (APQC 12.0)

**Meter Reading and Billing (APQC 12.1)**
- Traditional: Monthly manual meter reading
- AMI (Advanced Metering Infrastructure): Remote reading, 15-minute intervals
- Net metering: Rooftop solar generation credits for excess generation
- Time-of-use (TOU) rates: Different prices by time of day
- Critical peak pricing: Higher prices during demand peaks
- Demand charges: Fixed charge based on peak usage period

**Customer Service (APQC 12.2)**
- Multi-channel: Phone, online, mobile app, in-person
- Outage management: Outage reporting, restoration tracking
- Bill assistance: Payment plans, low-income programs
- Energy efficiency: Audit, rebates, weatherization programs
- Community engagement: Educational programs, stakeholder meetings

**Connections and Disconnections (APQC 12.3)**
- New service: Pole and service line installation
- Upgrade service: Increasing capacity for new loads
- Disconnection: Non-payment, end of service
- Reconnection: Restoring service after payment or completion
- Time commitments: Same day for new service (varies), 1-3 days standard

**Demand Response Programs (APQC 12.4-12.5)**
- Direct load control: Utility control of specific loads (AC, water heater)
- Voluntary reduction: Customer choice to reduce load during events
- Real-time pricing: Dynamic pricing based on wholesale market prices
- Aggregation: Combining many small loads for wholesale market participation
- Emerging: Vehicle-to-grid (V2G), water heating storage, battery systems
