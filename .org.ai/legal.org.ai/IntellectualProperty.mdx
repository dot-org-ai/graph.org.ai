# Intellectual Property Law

Intellectual property (IP) law protects creations of the mind including inventions, literary and artistic works, designs, symbols, names, and images used in commerce. IP law grants creators exclusive rights to use and commercialize their innovations and creative works, balancing incentives for innovation with public access to knowledge and culture.

IP legal practice encompasses four primary areas: patents (inventions and discoveries), trademarks (brands and source identifiers), copyrights (original works of authorship), and trade secrets (confidential business information). Attorneys advise on IP strategy, prosecute applications before government agencies, manage IP portfolios, negotiate licenses and transactions, and litigate infringement disputes.

## Overview

Intellectual property law covers multiple forms of protection:

**Patents**:
- Utility patents for functional inventions (20-year term)
- Design patents for ornamental designs (15-year term)
- Plant patents for new plant varieties (20-year term)
- Provisional and non-provisional applications
- Patent prosecution and examination
- Maintenance fees and patent term

**Trademarks**:
- Word marks, logos, slogans, and trade dress
- Federal registration with USPTO (Lanham Act)
- State common law trademark rights
- Collective marks and certification marks
- Trademark search, clearance, and prosecution
- Madrid Protocol international registration

**Copyrights**:
- Original works of authorship fixed in tangible medium
- Literary, musical, dramatic, artistic, and architectural works
- Sound recordings and motion pictures
- Software and computer programs
- Registration with U.S. Copyright Office
- Life of author plus 70 years (or 95 years for works for hire)

**Trade Secrets**:
- Confidential business information providing competitive advantage
- Customer lists, formulas, processes, methods
- Reasonable measures to maintain secrecy
- Indefinite duration if secrecy maintained
- State law protection (Uniform Trade Secrets Act)
- Federal protection (Defend Trade Secrets Act)

## Patent Law

### Patentability Requirements

**Statutory Categories (35 U.S.C. § 101)**:
- Processes (methods, algorithms, business methods)
- Machines (mechanical and electromechanical devices)
- Manufactures (articles of manufacture, compositions)
- Compositions of matter (chemical compounds, pharmaceuticals)
- Improvements to existing inventions

**Novelty (35 U.S.C. § 102)**:
- Not patented, described in publication, or publicly used before filing
- One-year grace period for inventor's own disclosures (U.S. only)
- First-inventor-to-file system (after America Invents Act 2013)
- Prior art includes worldwide publications and public use

**Non-Obviousness (35 U.S.C. § 103)**:
- Invention not obvious to person of ordinary skill in the art
- Consideration of prior art references in combination
- Secondary considerations (commercial success, long-felt need, failure of others)
- Graham factors analysis

**Utility (35 U.S.C. § 101)**:
- Specific, substantial, and credible utility
- Operable and capable of achieving stated purpose
- No perpetual motion machines or non-useful inventions

**Written Description and Enablement (35 U.S.C. § 112)**:
- Specification must describe invention in full, clear, concise, and exact terms
- Enable person of skill in the art to make and use invention
- Best mode requirement (no longer enforced post-AIA)
- Claims must particularly point out and distinctly claim the invention

### Patent Prosecution

**Provisional Applications**:
- Placeholder application establishing priority date
- 12-month pendency, does not mature to patent
- No claims required (specification only)
- Lower filing fee ($85 micro, $170 small, $340 large entity)
- Conversion to non-provisional within 12 months

**Non-Provisional Applications**:
- Complete application with specification, claims, drawings
- Examination by patent examiner
- Office actions and responses
- Amendments and continuation practice
- Allowance and issue fee

**Patent Examination Process**:
- Filing and formal examination
- Prior art search by examiner
- First office action (often rejection)
- Applicant's response and amendments
- Interview with examiner (optional)
- Final office action (if necessary)
- Notice of allowance or continued rejection
- Appeals to Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB)

**Claim Drafting**:
- Independent claims (standalone)
- Dependent claims (narrow and refer to other claims)
- Claim structure (preamble, transition, body)
- Means-plus-function claims (35 U.S.C. § 112(f))
- Product-by-process claims
- Jepson claims for improvements

**Continuation Practice**:
- **Continuation Application**: Same disclosure, different claims
- **Divisional Application**: Divided invention after restriction requirement
- **Continuation-in-Part (CIP)**: Adds new matter to disclosure
- Terminal disclaimers for obviousness-type double patenting

### Patent Validity Challenges

**Post-Grant Proceedings (PTAB)**:
- **Inter Partes Review (IPR)**: Challenge patents on prior art grounds (within 9 years of grant)
- **Post-Grant Review (PGR)**: Broader grounds within 9 months of grant
- **Covered Business Method (CBM) Review**: Financial services method patents
- **Ex Parte Reexamination**: USPTO reexamination based on prior art

**Invalidity Defenses in Litigation**:
- Anticipation by prior art (novelty failure)
- Obviousness in view of prior art
- Lack of written description or enablement
- Indefiniteness of claims
- Ineligible subject matter (abstract ideas, laws of nature)

### International Patent Protection

**Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)**:
- International application preserving foreign filing rights
- 30-month (or longer) deadline for national phase entry
- International search and preliminary examination
- Centralized filing and examination
- National/regional patent grants (not a worldwide patent)

**Foreign Filing Strategy**:
- Paris Convention 12-month priority claim
- Direct filing in foreign jurisdictions
- European Patent Convention (EPC) for Europe
- Costs and translation requirements
- Prosecution differences and patentability standards

**Patent Term and Maintenance**:
- 20 years from earliest non-provisional filing date
- Patent term adjustment (PTA) for USPTO delays
- Patent term extension (PTE) for regulatory review (FDA, etc.)
- Maintenance fees at 3.5, 7.5, and 11.5 years
- Failure to pay maintenance fees results in expiration

## Trademark Law

### Trademark Rights and Registration

**Trademark Distinctiveness**:
- **Fanciful**: Coined terms with no dictionary meaning (KODAK, XEROX)
- **Arbitrary**: Real words unrelated to goods/services (APPLE for computers)
- **Suggestive**: Suggests qualities but requires imagination (JAGUAR for cars)
- **Descriptive**: Describes goods/services (requires secondary meaning to register)
- **Generic**: Common name for goods/services (never protectable)

**Trademark Use in Commerce**:
- Actual use of mark in connection with goods or services
- Use must be bona fide, not token use
- Intent-to-use applications allowed (must show use before registration)
- Continuous use requirement to maintain rights

**Trademark Search and Clearance**:
- Knockout search of USPTO database
- Comprehensive search (federal, state, common law, domain names)
- Likelihood of confusion analysis
- Risk assessment and clearance opinion
- International trademark databases (TMview, WIPO Global Brand Database)

### Federal Trademark Registration

**Application Process**:
- TEAS filing (Standard or Plus)
- Identification of goods and services (Nice Classification)
- Specimen showing use in commerce
- Filing basis (use in commerce or intent to use)
- Examination by USPTO examining attorney

**Office Actions and Responses**:
- Likelihood of confusion refusals (similar marks)
- Merely descriptive refusals
- Ornamental use refusals
- Disclaimer requirements
- Amendments to application
- Responses to office actions (6-month deadline)

**Publication and Opposition**:
- Publication for opposition in Official Gazette
- 30-day opposition period (extendable)
- Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) proceedings
- Notice of allowance (if intent-to-use)
- Statement of use and registration

**Madrid Protocol International Registration**:
- Single application for multiple countries
- Based on U.S. application or registration
- Centralized management through WIPO
- Examination by national trademark offices
- Cost-effective international protection

### Trademark Maintenance

**Post-Registration Requirements**:
- Section 8 Declaration of Continued Use (between 5th-6th year)
- Section 8 and Section 9 Renewal (between 9th-10th year, then every 10 years)
- Section 15 Declaration of Incontestability (after 5 years)
- Specimens showing current use
- Failure to file results in cancellation

**Trademark Monitoring and Enforcement**:
- Watch services for new applications
- Policing marketplace for infringement
- Cease and desist letters
- Opposition and cancellation proceedings
- Customs recordation to stop imports

### Trademark Licensing and Transactions

**Trademark Licenses**:
- Exclusive vs. non-exclusive licenses
- Quality control provisions (essential to avoid abandonment)
- Royalty structure and payment terms
- Territory and field of use restrictions
- Term and termination provisions
- Co-branding and joint ventures

**Trademark Assignments**:
- Assignment of mark and goodwill
- With or without associated business
- Recording with USPTO
- International recordation
- Due diligence in acquisitions

## Copyright Law

### Copyrightable Subject Matter

**Original Works of Authorship**:
- Literary works (books, articles, software code)
- Musical works (compositions and lyrics)
- Dramatic works (plays, screenplays)
- Pantomimes and choreographic works
- Pictorial, graphic, and sculptural works
- Motion pictures and audiovisual works
- Sound recordings
- Architectural works

**Originality Requirement**:
- Work must be independently created (not copied)
- Minimal degree of creativity required
- Compilation and selection can be copyrightable
- Facts and ideas not copyrightable (only expression)

**Fixation in Tangible Medium**:
- Must be fixed in physical or electronic form
- Sufficiently permanent or stable
- Perceivable directly or with aid of machine
- Unfixed performances and improvisations not protected

**Works Not Copyrightable**:
- Ideas, concepts, procedures, methods, systems
- Facts and data (compilations may be copyrightable)
- Titles, names, short phrases, slogans
- Government works and public domain materials
- Useful articles (functional aspects)

### Copyright Registration

**Registration Process**:
- Application filed with U.S. Copyright Office
- Online filing (eCO system) or paper Form CO
- Deposit of work (two copies of best edition)
- Filing fee ($35-$65 depending on type)
- Examination and registration certificate

**Benefits of Registration**:
- Prerequisite to infringement suit for U.S. works
- Prima facie evidence of validity if registered within 5 years
- Statutory damages and attorneys' fees available
- Registration within 3 months of publication or before infringement
- Customs recordation for import protection

**Publication Status**:
- Published works: distributed to public
- Unpublished works: not distributed to public
- Different deposit requirements
- Publication affects copyright term for pre-1978 works

### Copyright Ownership

**Authorship and Ownership**:
- Author is initial owner of copyright
- Joint works: co-authors with intent to merge contributions
- Collective works: separate copyrights in contributions and compilation
- Derivative works: separate copyright in new material

**Work Made for Hire**:
- Employee works: owned by employer (within scope of employment)
- Commissioned works: in nine statutory categories with written agreement
- Important for transfer of ownership to commissioning party
- No termination rights for works made for hire

**Transfer and Licensing**:
- Exclusive licenses must be in writing and signed
- Non-exclusive licenses can be oral or implied
- Assignment of copyright ownership
- Divisibility of rights (by medium, territory, time)
- Termination of transfers after 35 years (inalienable right)

### Fair Use and Limitations

**Fair Use Factors (17 U.S.C. § 107)**:
- Purpose and character of use (transformative vs. commercial)
- Nature of copyrighted work (creative vs. factual)
- Amount and substantiality of portion used
- Effect on potential market for work

**Fair Use Examples**:
- Criticism, comment, and news reporting
- Teaching and scholarship
- Research and library uses
- Parody and transformative uses

**Other Limitations**:
- First sale doctrine (resale of lawfully acquired copies)
- Library and archive exceptions
- Performance and display in classrooms
- Public domain works (expired copyright)

### Copyright Infringement

**Elements of Infringement**:
- Valid copyright ownership
- Copying of protected expression
- Improper appropriation (substantial similarity)
- Access to copyrighted work

**Direct vs. Indirect Infringement**:
- Direct infringement: copying, distribution, public performance, display
- Contributory infringement: knowingly facilitating infringement
- Vicarious infringement: right and ability to control, financial benefit

**Remedies**:
- Injunctive relief (preliminary and permanent)
- Actual damages and profits
- Statutory damages ($750-$30,000 per work, up to $150,000 for willful)
- Attorneys' fees and costs (in court's discretion)
- Impoundment and destruction of infringing articles

### Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA)

**Anti-Circumvention Provisions**:
- Prohibition on circumventing technological protection measures
- Trafficking in circumvention devices
- Exceptions for encryption research, security testing, reverse engineering

**Safe Harbor for Online Service Providers**:
- Limitation on liability for user-generated content
- Notice and takedown procedure
- Designated agent for DMCA notices
- Repeat infringer policy requirement
- No duty to monitor content

**DMCA Takedown Process**:
- Copyright owner sends takedown notice to service provider
- Service provider removes allegedly infringing content
- User can submit counter-notice claiming lawful use
- Copyright owner must file lawsuit within 10-14 days or content restored

## Trade Secret Law

### Trade Secret Protection

**Definition and Requirements**:
- Information with independent economic value from secrecy
- Not generally known or readily ascertainable
- Reasonable efforts to maintain secrecy
- Can include formulas, patterns, compilations, programs, devices, methods, techniques, processes

**Examples of Trade Secrets**:
- Coca-Cola formula and recipes
- Customer lists and pricing information
- Manufacturing processes and know-how
- Source code and algorithms (if not publicly available)
- Business strategies and plans
- Research and development data

**Reasonable Measures to Protect Secrecy**:
- Non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) with employees and partners
- Physical security (locked facilities, access controls)
- Digital security (passwords, encryption, access logs)
- Confidentiality policies and employee training
- Marking documents as confidential
- Exit interviews and return of materials

### Trade Secret Enforcement

**Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA)**:
- Adopted by 48 states (plus D.C. and territories)
- Defines trade secrets and misappropriation
- Injunctive relief and damages
- Attorneys' fees for willful and malicious misappropriation
- 3-year statute of limitations (generally)

**Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 (DTSA)**:
- Federal civil cause of action for trade secret misappropriation
- Ex parte seizure orders for extraordinary circumstances
- Injunctive relief, damages, and attorneys' fees
- Whistleblower immunity provision
- Notice requirement in employment agreements

**Misappropriation**:
- Acquisition by improper means (theft, espionage, breach)
- Disclosure or use without consent by one who knew or should have known
- Inevitable disclosure doctrine (minority of jurisdictions)

**Remedies**:
- Temporary restraining orders (TRO) and preliminary injunctions
- Permanent injunctive relief (time-limited)
- Actual damages and unjust enrichment
- Exemplary damages for willful and malicious (up to 2x under DTSA)
- Attorneys' fees for bad faith or willful and malicious

### Employee Mobility and Non-Competes

**Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs)**:
- Confidentiality obligations during and after employment
- Definition of confidential information
- Exclusions (public information, independently developed)
- Duration of obligations
- Remedies for breach

**Non-Compete Agreements**:
- Restriction on competing employment or business
- Reasonable in scope, geography, and duration
- Enforceable in most states (some states ban or limit)
- California generally prohibits (with narrow exceptions)
- Consideration required at hiring or later

**Non-Solicitation Agreements**:
- Prohibition on soliciting customers or employees
- More likely to be enforced than non-competes
- Reasonable scope and duration required
- Customer non-solicitation vs. employee non-solicitation

**Inevitable Disclosure**:
- Injunction based on likelihood employee will inevitably use/disclose trade secrets
- Recognized in minority of jurisdictions
- Requires showing of bad faith or actual threatened disclosure
- Alternative to non-compete enforcement

## IP Litigation

### Patent Litigation

**Venue and Jurisdiction**:
- Eastern District of Texas (historically popular)
- Delaware and Northern District of California
- Patent Case Management Pilot Program courts
- Forum selection clauses
- Transfer of venue (28 U.S.C. § 1404)

**Patent Infringement Claims**:
- Literal infringement of patent claims
- Doctrine of equivalents (functionally equivalent)
- Inducement and contributory infringement
- Willful infringement (enhanced damages up to 3x)

**Defenses to Infringement**:
- Non-infringement (claim construction)
- Invalidity (anticipation, obviousness, lack of enablement)
- Unenforceability (inequitable conduct, patent misuse)
- Exhaustion and implied license

**Claim Construction (Markman Hearing)**:
- Interpretation of claim terms
- Intrinsic evidence (claims, specification, prosecution history)
- Extrinsic evidence (expert testimony, dictionaries, treatises)
- Court determines claim meaning (not jury)
- Often dispositive of infringement and validity

**Patent Damages**:
- Lost profits (but-for causation)
- Reasonable royalty (hypothetical negotiation)
- Entire market value rule vs. smallest salable unit
- Georgia-Pacific factors for reasonable royalty
- Enhanced damages for willful infringement

### Trademark Litigation

**Likelihood of Confusion**:
- Similarity of marks (sight, sound, meaning)
- Similarity of goods/services
- Trade channels and purchaser sophistication
- Strength of mark and fame
- Intent and actual confusion
- DuPont or Sleekcraft factors (depending on circuit)

**Dilution Claims**:
- Famous marks only (federal)
- Dilution by blurring (weakening distinctiveness)
- Dilution by tarnishment (harming reputation)
- No likelihood of confusion required
- Parody defense

**False Advertising**:
- Lanham Act Section 43(a) claims
- Literal falsity or implied misleading
- Material misrepresentation
- Interstate commerce
- Likelihood of injury
- Corrective advertising as remedy

**Trademark Remedies**:
- Injunctive relief (preliminary and permanent)
- Damages (profits, actual damages)
- Attorneys' fees for exceptional cases
- Destruction of infringing materials
- Corrective advertising

### Copyright Litigation

**Infringement Analysis**:
- Ownership of valid copyright
- Copying (direct evidence or access + substantial similarity)
- Improper appropriation
- Extrinsic test (objective similarity)
- Intrinsic test (subjective similarity to ordinary observer)

**Digital Copyright Issues**:
- DMCA safe harbor for online platforms
- Peer-to-peer file sharing liability
- Streaming and cloud storage services
- User-generated content platforms
- Automated content filtering (Content ID)

**Software Copyright**:
- Protection of source code and object code
- Non-literal elements (structure, sequence, organization)
- Abstraction-filtration-comparison test
- Merger doctrine (expression merges with idea)
- Scenes a faire doctrine (stock or standard elements)
