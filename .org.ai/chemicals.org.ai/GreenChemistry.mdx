# Green Chemistry

Green chemistry, also known as sustainable chemistry, is the design of chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. It represents a paradigm shift from traditional chemical manufacturing toward environmentally responsible and economically viable practices.

## Overview

Green chemistry is driven by:
- **Environmental imperative**: Climate change, pollution, resource depletion
- **Regulatory pressure**: Stricter environmental regulations globally
- **Economic opportunity**: Reduced waste, energy efficiency, new markets
- **Social responsibility**: Health and safety, corporate sustainability commitments
- **Resource scarcity**: Finite petroleum reserves, need for renewable alternatives

**Market growth**:
- Bio-based chemicals: $100+ billion market, 10-15% CAGR
- Green solvents: $5 billion market
- Renewable materials: 5-10% of chemical production by 2030
- Carbon capture utilization: Emerging multi-billion dollar opportunity

The transition to green chemistry is essential for achieving:
- UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
- Paris Agreement climate targets
- Circular economy principles
- Zero waste manufacturing

## The Twelve Principles of Green Chemistry

Developed by Paul Anastas and John Warner (1998), these principles guide sustainable chemical design:

### 1. Prevention (Waste Prevention)

**Principle**: It is better to prevent waste than to treat or clean up waste after it is created.

**Implementation**:
- Atom-efficient reactions (maximize incorporation of reactants into product)
- Minimize by-products and waste streams
- Process intensification to reduce material and energy use
- Closed-loop systems with solvent and catalyst recovery

**Example**:
- **Traditional**: Adipic acid production generates N₂O (greenhouse gas, 310× CO₂)
- **Green**: Glucose fermentation to adipic acid (zero N₂O emissions)

### 2. Atom Economy

**Principle**: Synthetic methods should maximize the incorporation of all materials used in the process into the final product.

**Calculation**: Atom Economy = (MW of desired product / MW of all reactants) × 100%

**Implementation**:
- Favor addition reactions over substitution
- Use catalytic processes
- Avoid stoichiometric reagents that end up in waste
- Design reactions with high selectivity

**Example**:
- **Poor atom economy**: Grignard reactions (stoichiometric magnesium salts as waste)
- **High atom economy**: Hydrogenation (H₂ adds to molecule, nearly 100%)

### 3. Less Hazardous Chemical Syntheses

**Principle**: Design syntheses to use and generate substances with little or no toxicity to human health and the environment.

**Implementation**:
- Replace toxic reagents (phosgene, chlorine, formaldehyde)
- Avoid carcinogens, mutagens, reproductive toxins
- Minimize acute toxicity
- Design for biodegradability

**Example**:
- **Traditional polycarbonate**: Bisphenol A + phosgene (highly toxic)
- **Green route**: BPA + diphenyl carbonate (melt transesterification, no phosgene)

### 4. Designing Safer Chemicals

**Principle**: Chemical products should be designed to preserve efficacy while reducing toxicity.

**Implementation**:
- Structure-activity relationship (SAR) modeling
- Avoid persistent, bioaccumulative, toxic (PBT) substances
- Design for degradation
- Minimize endocrine disruption

**Example**:
- **Safer pesticides**: Spinosad (natural fermentation product, low mammalian toxicity) vs. organophosphates
- **Biodegradable surfactants**: Alcohol ethoxylates vs. persistent alkylphenol ethoxylates

### 5. Safer Solvents and Auxiliaries

**Principle**: Use auxiliary substances (solvents, separation agents) that are innocuous when possible.

**Implementation**:
- **Water as solvent**: Aqueous reactions when feasible
- **Supercritical CO₂**: Extraction, polymerization, dry cleaning
- **Ionic liquids**: Non-volatile, recyclable, tunable properties
- **Bio-based solvents**: Ethyl lactate, cyrene, 2-methyltetrahydrofuran
- **Solvent-free reactions**: Neat reactions, solid-state synthesis
- **Solvent recovery and recycling**: Distillation, membrane separation

**Green Solvent Selection Guides**:
- GSK Solvent Selection Guide
- CHEM21 Solvent Selection Tool
- ACS GCI Pharmaceutical Roundtable Solvent Tool

**Example**:
- **Traditional**: Hexane for edible oil extraction (flammable, neurotoxic)
- **Green**: Supercritical CO₂ extraction (non-toxic, recyclable)

### 6. Design for Energy Efficiency

**Principle**: Energy requirements should be minimized. Synthetic methods should be conducted at ambient temperature and pressure when possible.

**Implementation**:
- **Catalysis**: Lower activation energy, reduce temperature
- **Process intensification**: Microreactors, continuous flow
- **Heat integration**: Recover and reuse process heat
- **Renewable energy**: Solar, wind, biomass for chemical processes
- **Exothermic reactions**: Design to utilize reaction heat

**Example**:
- **Traditional Haber-Bosch**: 450°C, 200 bar (energy-intensive)
- **Goal**: Electrocatalytic nitrogen reduction at ambient conditions (under development)

### 7. Use of Renewable Feedstocks

**Principle**: Use raw materials and feedstocks that are renewable rather than depleting.

**Implementation**:
- **Biomass**: Sugars, starch, cellulose, lignin, oils
- **Agricultural waste**: Corn stover, bagasse, forestry residues
- **Algae**: Biofuels, omega-3 fatty acids
- **CO₂ utilization**: Carbon capture and conversion to chemicals
- **Waste streams**: Food waste, municipal solid waste

**Bio-Based Platforms**:
- C5 sugars (xylose) → furfural → solvents, resins
- C6 sugars (glucose) → lactic acid → PLA, solvents
- Glycerol (biodiesel by-product) → propylene glycol, epichlorohydrin
- Vegetable oils → fatty acids, polyols

**Example**:
- **Bio-PET**: 30% bio-content (bio-ethylene glycol from sugarcane ethanol)
- **PLA (Polylactic Acid)**: 100% bio-based from corn or sugarcane

### 8. Reduce Derivatives

**Principle**: Unnecessary derivatization (blocking groups, protection/deprotection) should be avoided.

**Implementation**:
- Direct functionalization
- C-H activation instead of pre-functionalized substrates
- One-pot multi-step syntheses
- Telescoping reactions (no isolation of intermediates)

**Example**:
- **Traditional synthesis**: Requires protection of hydroxyl groups, multiple steps
- **Green chemistry**: Direct catalytic C-H functionalization (no protecting groups)

### 9. Catalysis

**Principle**: Catalytic reagents are superior to stoichiometric reagents.

**Implementation**:
- **Homogeneous catalysis**: Transition metal complexes, organocatalysts
- **Heterogeneous catalysis**: Solid catalysts (zeolites, metal oxides, supported metals)
- **Biocatalysis**: Enzymes, whole-cell fermentation
- **Photocatalysis**: Light-driven reactions
- **Electrocatalysis**: Electrochemical synthesis

**Advantages**:
- Sub-stoichiometric quantities (0.01-5 mol%)
- Reduced waste
- Higher selectivity
- Milder conditions

**Example**:
- **Stoichiometric oxidation**: Chromium(VI) reagents (toxic waste)
- **Catalytic oxidation**: Palladium catalyst with O₂ (air oxidation)

### 10. Design for Degradation

**Principle**: Chemical products should be designed to break down into innocuous degradation products at the end of their function.

**Implementation**:
- **Biodegradable polymers**: PLA, PHA, starch blends
- **Hydrolyzable linkages**: Esters, amides, acetals
- **Avoid persistent structures**: No halogenated aromatics, long-chain PFAs
- **Design for composting or biological treatment**

**Example**:
- **PLA (Polylactic Acid)**: Composts in industrial facilities (55-60°C)
- **PHA (Polyhydroxyalkanoates)**: Biodegrades in soil and marine environments

### 11. Real-Time Analysis for Pollution Prevention

**Principle**: Analytical methodologies need to be developed to allow real-time, in-process monitoring and control.

**Implementation**:
- **Process Analytical Technology (PAT)**: In-line sensors
- **Spectroscopy**: NIR, Raman, IR for real-time composition
- **Advanced process control (APC)**: Optimize reaction conditions
- **AI/ML**: Predictive models to prevent off-spec production and waste

**Example**:
- **In-line NIR spectroscopy**: Monitor reaction completion, avoid over-reaction and by-products
- **Real-time pH and temperature control**: Prevent runaway reactions

### 12. Inherently Safer Chemistry for Accident Prevention

**Principle**: Substances and the form of a substance used in a chemical process should minimize the potential for chemical accidents.

**Implementation**:
- **Minimize hazardous inventories**: Smaller batch sizes, continuous flow
- **Substitute hazardous materials**: Less toxic, flammable, or explosive
- **Moderate conditions**: Lower temperature, pressure, concentrations
- **Design fail-safe**: Automatic shutdown, pressure relief, containment

**Example**:
- **Continuous flow reactors**: Small reaction volumes, better heat transfer, safer for exothermic reactions
- **Replace phosgene**: Safer carbonyl sources (dimethyl carbonate)

## Bio-Based Chemicals and Materials

### Biomass Feedstocks

**Lignocellulosic Biomass**:
- **Cellulose** (40-50%): Glucose polymer
  - Hydrolysis → glucose → platform chemicals
- **Hemicellulose** (25-35%): Mixed sugars (xylose, arabinose, mannose)
  - Furfural, xylitol
- **Lignin** (15-20%): Aromatic polymer
  - Phenolic compounds, carbon fibers, aromatic chemicals

**Sources**:
- Agricultural residues (corn stover, wheat straw, rice husks)
- Forestry residues (sawdust, wood chips)
- Dedicated energy crops (switchgrass, miscanthus)
- Municipal solid waste (paper, cardboard)

**Sugars and Starch**:
- Corn, sugarcane, sugar beets
- Cassava, potatoes
- First-generation biorefinery feedstocks

**Vegetable Oils and Fats**:
- Soybean, palm, rapeseed, sunflower
- Castor oil (ricinoleic acid)
- Animal fats, waste cooking oil

**Algae and Cyanobacteria**:
- High lipid content (20-50%)
- Rapid growth, non-food crops
- CO₂ sequestration

### Platform Chemicals from Biomass

**Lactic Acid** - 1M+ tons/year
- Production: Glucose fermentation (Lactobacillus, engineered strains)
- Derivatives:
  - **PLA (Polylactic Acid)**: Biodegradable polymer
  - **Acrylic acid**: Polymerization via lactide dehydration
  - **Propylene glycol**: Hydrogenation
  - **Ethyl lactate**: Green solvent

**Succinic Acid** - 50,000+ tons/year (bio-based)
- Production: Glucose fermentation (engineered E. coli, yeast)
- Derivatives:
  - **1,4-Butanediol (BDO)**: Hydrogenation → polyesters, polyurethanes
  - **Tetrahydrofuran (THF)**: Solvent, polyether
  - **Gamma-butyrolactone (GBL)**: Solvent
- **PBS (Polybutylene Succinate)**: Biodegradable polyester

**Furfural** - 300,000 tons/year
- Production: Xylose (C5 sugars from hemicellulose) dehydration
  - Acid catalyst, 150-200°C
- Derivatives:
  - **Furfuryl alcohol**: Resins for foundry sand binders
  - **2-Methylfuran**: Biofuel
  - **Furoic acid**: Chemical intermediate

**5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF)**
- Production: Glucose or fructose dehydration
  - Acid catalyst
- Derivatives:
  - **2,5-Furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA)**: Polyester monomer
  - **PEF (Polyethylene Furanoate)**: Bio-based PET alternative (superior barrier)
  - **Levulinic acid**: Platform chemical

**Levulinic Acid**
- Production: C6 sugars via acid hydrolysis
- Derivatives:
  - **Methyltetrahydrofuran (MTHF)**: Green solvent
  - **Gamma-valerolactone (GVL)**: Solvent, fuel additive
  - **Diphenolic acid**: BPA replacement

**Adipic Acid** (bio-route)
- Production: Glucose fermentation → muconic acid → hydrogenation
- Traditional route: Nitric acid oxidation of cyclohexane (N₂O emissions)
- Bio-route: Zero N₂O emissions

**Itaconic Acid**
- Production: Glucose fermentation (Aspergillus terreus)
- Derivatives:
  - Polymers (superabsorbents, resins)
  - Replacement for acrylic acid in some applications

**Glycerol** (biodiesel by-product)
- Production: 10% by weight from biodiesel (FAME) production
- Surplus: Market oversupply
- Derivatives:
  - **Propylene glycol**: Hydrogenolysis
  - **Epichlorohydrin**: Dehydrochlorination → epoxy resins
  - **1,3-Propanediol**: Polytrimethylene terephthalate (PTT)
  - **Acrolein**: Dehydration → acrylic acid

### Bio-Based Polymers

**Polylactic Acid (PLA)** - 400,000+ tons/year
- Monomer: Lactic acid from glucose fermentation
- Polymerization: Ring-opening polymerization of lactide (cyclic dimer)
- Properties:
  - Tg: 55-65°C (limited heat resistance)
  - Transparent, rigid
  - Biodegradable (industrial composting, 55-60°C)
- Applications:
  - Packaging films, containers
  - Disposable serviceware
  - 3D printing filament
  - Textiles (apparel, non-wovens)
- Limitations: Low heat deflection, brittle

**Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA)** - 10,000+ tons/year
- Production: Bacterial fermentation (intracellular storage polymer)
  - Cupriavidus necator, engineered E. coli
- Types:
  - **PHB (Polyhydroxybutyrate)**: Stiff, brittle
  - **PHBV (Poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate))**: More flexible
  - **PHBHHx**: Elastomeric
- Properties:
  - Biodegradable in soil, compost, marine environments
  - Similar to PP in properties
- Applications:
  - Packaging, agriculture (mulch film)
  - Medical (sutures, implants)
  - Personal care
- Challenges: High cost ($3-5/kg vs. $1-2/kg for PLA)

**Bio-Polyethylene (Bio-PE)** - 300,000+ tons/year
- Production: Sugarcane ethanol → ethylene → polymerization
- Properties: Identical to fossil-based PE
- Drop-in replacement: No infrastructure changes
- Not biodegradable (same as fossil PE)
- Carbon-neutral claim (biogenic carbon)
- Major producer: Braskem (Brazil)

**Bio-Polypropylene (Bio-PP)**
- Production: Bio-ethanol → ethylene → propylene (various routes)
  - Or bio-based propane dehydrogenation
- Under development, limited commercial production
- Identical properties to fossil PP

**Bio-PET**
- 30% bio-content: Bio-ethylene glycol (from sugarcane ethanol)
- 100% bio-PET: Bio-EG + bio-PTA (from bio-p-xylene)
  - Full bio-PET under development
- Coca-Cola PlantBottle: 30% bio-PET

**Polyethylene Furanoate (PEF)**
- Monomers: 2,5-Furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) + ethylene glycol
- 100% bio-based
- Superior properties to PET:
  - Better gas barrier (3-10× CO₂, O₂)
  - Higher mechanical strength
  - Lower permeability
- Applications: Bottles, packaging films
- Commercialization: Avantium (NL)

**Bio-Polyamides (Bio-Nylon)**
- **PA 11 (Nylon 11)**: Castor oil → 11-aminoundecanoic acid
  - Arkema Rilsan, long-established bio-nylon
- **PA 610**: Bio-sebacic acid (castor oil) + hexamethylene diamine
- **PA 56**: Pentamethylene diamine (bio-based) + adipic acid

**Starch-Based Plastics**
- Thermoplastic starch (TPS): Plasticized starch (glycerol)
- Blends: TPS + PLA, TPS + PBAT
- Applications: Bags, packaging peanuts, disposable serviceware
- Fully biodegradable

**Cellulose-Based Materials**
- **Cellulose acetate**: Films, fibers, cigarette filters
- **Regenerated cellulose (Rayon)**: Viscose process
- **Cellophane**: Transparent films
- **Nanocellulose**: Reinforcement, barrier coatings, composites

**PBAT (Polybutylene Adipate Terephthalate)**
- Copolyester: Adipic acid + terephthalic acid + 1,4-butanediol
- Biodegradable (can be partially bio-based if bio-BDO used)
- Applications: Compostable bags, mulch film
- Often blended with PLA or starch

### Biocatalysis and Fermentation

**Enzymes as Catalysts**
- Specificity: Chemo-, regio-, and enantioselectivity
- Mild conditions: Aqueous, ambient temperature, neutral pH
- Biodegradable catalysts
- Challenges: Stability, cost, activity in non-natural substrates

**Industrial Enzymes**:
- **Lipases**: Ester hydrolysis, transesterification (biodiesel)
- **Proteases**: Peptide bond cleavage (detergents, food)
- **Amylases**: Starch hydrolysis (HFCS, ethanol)
- **Cellulases**: Cellulose hydrolysis (biofuels)
- **Laccases**: Lignin degradation, oxidation
- **Transaminases**: Chiral amine synthesis (pharmaceuticals)
- **Nitrile hydratases**: Acrylamide production

**Whole-Cell Fermentation**:
- Engineered microorganisms (E. coli, yeast, bacteria)
- Multi-step enzymatic pathways
- Examples:
  - Lactic acid, succinic acid, itaconic acid
  - 1,3-Propanediol (DuPont, from corn glucose)
  - Artemisinin (antimalarial, from engineered yeast)

**Synthetic Biology**:
- Metabolic engineering for novel pathways
- CRISPR genome editing
- Cell-free systems

### Carbon Capture and Utilization (CCU)

**CO₂ as Feedstock**:
- Abundant, non-toxic, renewable carbon source
- Challenges: Thermodynamic stability (low reactivity), energy input

**CO₂ Conversion Technologies**:

1. **Methanol from CO₂**
   - CO₂ + 3H₂ → CH₃OH + H₂O
   - Renewable H₂ from electrolysis
   - Carbon Recycling International (Iceland), Mitsui Chemicals

2. **Formic Acid**
   - CO₂ + H₂ → HCOOH
   - Energy storage, fuel cells

3. **CO₂-Based Polymers**
   - **Polycarbonates**: CO₂ + epoxides → aliphatic polycarbonates
     - Covestro, Saudi Aramco (cardyon process)
   - **Polyurethanes**: CO₂-based polyols
   - **PPC (Poly(propylene carbonate))**: CO₂ + propylene oxide

4. **Mineralization**
   - CO₂ + calcium/magnesium oxides → carbonates
   - Building materials (concrete, aggregates)
   - CarbonCure, Solidia

5. **Electrochemical Reduction**
   - CO₂ + H₂O + electricity → CO, formate, ethylene, methanol
   - Renewable electricity (solar, wind)
   - Under development (low efficiency, high cost)

6. **Photocatalytic Reduction**
   - Artificial photosynthesis
   - CO₂ + H₂O + light → fuels, chemicals

7. **Biological Fixation**
   - Algae cultivation → biomass → biofuels, chemicals
   - Engineered bacteria (CO₂ to products)

## Green Solvents

### Water
- Ultimate green solvent
- Applications: Aqueous reactions, biphasic catalysis
- Limitations: Low solubility of organics, high heat capacity

### Supercritical CO₂ (scCO₂)
- Critical point: 31°C, 73 bar
- Properties: Gas-like diffusion, liquid-like solvation
- Applications:
  - Extraction (caffeine decaffeination, hop extraction)
  - Dry cleaning
  - Polymer processing (foaming, impregnation)
  - Reaction medium

### Ionic Liquids (ILs)
- Salts liquid at <100°C
- Tunable properties (anion/cation combinations)
- Non-volatile, non-flammable
- Applications:
  - Cellulose dissolution
  - Electrochemistry (batteries)
  - CO₂ capture
- Challenges: Cost, toxicity (some), biodegradability

### Bio-Based Solvents

**Ethyl Lactate**
- From lactic acid (bio-based)
- Biodegradable, low toxicity
- Applications: Coatings, cleaners, electronics

**Cyrene (Dihydrolevoglucosenone)**
- From cellulose
- Dipolar aprotic solvent (replacement for NMP, DMF)
- Pharmaceutical synthesis

**2-Methyltetrahydrofuran (2-MeTHF)**
- From levulinic acid (biomass)
- Replacement for THF
- Pharmaceutical industry

**Limonene**
- From citrus peel waste
- Solvent, degreaser, fragrance

**Glycerol**
- Biodiesel by-product
- High boiling, polar solvent
- Catalyst support

### Deep Eutectic Solvents (DES)
- Mixtures of hydrogen bond donor and acceptor
- Example: Choline chloride + urea
- Low cost, biodegradable, tunable
- Applications: Extraction, electrochemistry, biomass processing

## Energy Efficiency and Process Intensification

### Continuous Flow Chemistry
- Microreactors and flow reactors
- Advantages:
  - Better heat and mass transfer
  - Precise control (temperature, residence time)
  - Safer (small volumes, no accumulation of hazardous intermediates)
  - Scalable (numbering up)
- Applications: Pharmaceuticals, fine chemicals, dangerous reactions

### Microwave-Assisted Synthesis
- Rapid, uniform heating
- Shorter reaction times
- Energy efficiency
- Selective heating (polar molecules absorb microwaves)

### Ultrasound-Assisted Reactions
- Acoustic cavitation enhances mixing and reactivity
- Faster reactions, milder conditions
- Applications: Emulsification, crystallization, extraction

### Ball Milling (Mechanochemistry)
- Solvent-free synthesis
- Grinding induces chemical reactions
- Applications: Organic synthesis, materials, pharmaceuticals

### Photochemistry and Photocatalysis
- Light as reagent (UV, visible, solar)
- Photocatalysts: TiO₂, dye-sensitized catalysts
- Applications: Oxidations, reductions, C-C coupling

### Electrochemistry
- Electrons as reagents (oxidations, reductions)
- Renewable electricity
- Applications: Adiponitrile (nylon), organic synthesis

## Circular Economy in Chemicals

### Design for Recycling
- Mono-material packaging
- Avoid multi-layer laminates
- Recyclable polymers (PET, HDPE, PP)

### Chemical Recycling (Advanced Recycling)
- **Pyrolysis**: Plastics → oil → steam cracker → virgin-quality monomers
- **Depolymerization**: PET glycolysis, PC methanolysis
- **Gasification**: Waste → syngas → chemicals
- Examples: Eastman, Agilyx, Loop Industries, Carbios

### Industrial Symbiosis
- One industry's waste = another's feedstock
- **Kalundborg, Denmark**: Industrial ecology park
  - Power plant steam → pharmaceutical production
  - Gypsum → wallboard manufacturing
  - Fly ash → cement

### Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)
- Manufacturers responsible for end-of-life
- Funding recycling infrastructure
- Design incentives for recyclability

## Challenges and Future Directions

### Cost Competitiveness
- Bio-based chemicals often 1.5-3× cost of petrochemicals
- Economies of scale needed
- Carbon pricing would improve competitiveness

### Feedstock Availability
- Food vs. fuel vs. materials debate
- Lignocellulosic biomass: Not yet cost-competitive
- Algae: Scalability challenges

### Technology Maturity
- Many green technologies at pilot or demo scale
- Scale-up risks and capital requirements
- Longer payback periods

### Performance Trade-Offs
- Bio-based materials may have inferior properties
- Example: PLA heat resistance vs. PET

### Infrastructure and Supply Chain
- Existing infrastructure optimized for petrochemicals
- Biorefineries require new infrastructure
- Drop-in bio-based chemicals easier to adopt

### Regulatory and Certification
- Life cycle assessment (LCA) standardization
- Certification schemes (USDA BioPreferred, OK biobased)
- Biodegradability standards (ASTM D6400, EN 13432)

### Future Innovations
- **Artificial intelligence**: Accelerate green chemistry design
- **Synthetic biology**: Novel metabolic pathways
- **Electrification**: Renewable electricity for chemical synthesis
- **Modular biorefineries**: Distributed production
- **Waste valorization**: Convert waste to high-value chemicals

## Policy and Incentives

### Government Support
- **Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS)**: U.S. biofuels mandate
- **EU Renewable Energy Directive**: Biofuels, bio-based targets
- **Tax credits**: Biodiesel blender credit, cellulosic biofuel credit
- **R&D funding**: DOE, EU Horizon, national programs

### Carbon Pricing
- Carbon tax or cap-and-trade
- Levels playing field for bio-based vs. fossil-based

### Procurement Policies
- Government preference for bio-based products
- USDA BioPreferred program

### Circular Economy Regulations
- Extended producer responsibility mandates
- Recycled content requirements
- Single-use plastic bans
